What the chase costs

Here is what nobody at the tournament is going to tell you.

The average family spent $1,016 on their kid's main sport in 2024. That is from a survey of 1,848 youth sports parents by the Aspen Institute's Project Play, and it is up 46 percent in five years. Basketball was worse than average. The same survey found the average basketball parent spent 105 percent more on their kid in 2024 than in 2019. The price of chasing doubled, and the number of D1 roster spots did not.

That $1,016 is the average across every kid in every sport, most of them nine years old. It is not what a family chasing a scholarship spends. A reporter who tracked AAU expenses this summer put program fees alone at $800 to more than $1,000, one three-day showcase weekend at about $1,000 for one parent and one player, and a July of out-of-state live period events at roughly $6,000 for a family of two. Add the trainer. Add the film subscription so you can watch the games you could not drive to, at $25 a day or $50 an event. He put a full season at roughly $10,000, and families told him they spend more than that.

Now add the back end. If the plan works the way the AAU coach described it, and your son takes a "preferred walk-on" spot at an out-of-state D1, the College Board's sticker price for that is $31,880 a year in tuition and fees before he eats or sleeps anywhere. For a seat on the bench. Behind a 22-year-old transfer who already scored in college games.

Do the math on your own receipts tonight. Four years of travel ball. A prep year if somebody talks you into one. The walk-on tuition. Write the number down. Then ask the question this book is built on: what if he could have been playing college basketball, on scholarship money, at a school that called him in October, for a fraction of that?

That is not a hypothetical. In 2024-25, 19,617 young men played NCAA basketball. About 6,000 of them played Division I. The rest played D2 and D3, and NAIA and junior college are on top of that. Most of the kids playing college basketball tonight are not playing D1, and most of them were not chasing it either. Their families found the door that was open.

It is a kid who was the best player on every team he ever played on, sitting in a gym in July watching a coach leave at halftime. It is the walk-on year, the one you paid $31,880 for, where he practices every day and plays nine minutes all season and comes home in May asking whether he was ever any good.

He was. The plan was wrong, not the kid.

Some of you are reading this with a verbal commitment in hand and thinking it does not apply to you. Here is what I tell those families.

There are too many variables when it just has a verbal commitment. Coaches change, it could be assistant coach, staff changes, somebody they connected with. A verbal is a conversation. The early signing period this year ran November 11 through 18. The regular period opens April 14. Until something is signed and the aid agreement is in writing, your son is unsigned, and unsigned is a status, not a plan.

The film and the email, in my words

Two things get a kid recruited below the top 50 programs. Film a coach will open. An email the coach answers. The Playbook spends two full sections on them, Section 6 and Section 8, and here is the short version.

On paying for a highlight reel

It's not needed. They're going to want full game film when they're interested, and that speaks for itself, because you're going to have bad plays too, and they want to see those bad plays and how you respond.

That is the whole rule. A two-minute reel gets the door open. The full game is what the coach actually watches. Section 6 gives you the two-minute structure, what to cut, what to leave in, and how to send the full game link so the coach can find the bad plays himself.

On what the coach is looking at

Here is what college coaches have told me they look for first, standing next to me at events:

They look to see how they respond to their teammates, to the situation that the game is in, composure, enthusiasm, hustle, you know, just what their demeanor is.

Not the dunk. Not the step-back three. How he acts when the play goes wrong. That is why I want the bad plays in the film. A coach who sees your son sprint back after a turnover has learned more than any highlight could tell him.

On who writes the email

Here is the one that stings. A parent says the kid will not write the email himself.

then they're probably not very motivated, because at 5 a.m. they can't just write an email, something they're serious about. They're also not going to get up for the workout.

The email comes from your son. Not from you. Not from a service. Section 8 has the template he fills in, and the parent's job is to proofread it and hit nothing else.

On what goes in it

The template is good. It really just says I'm very interested in your program. I think I could be a great fit. And maybe one thing about your best attribute as a teammate, community member, school member, anything that will show your personality also, then let your film speak for itself.

Four sentences. A link. His coach's phone number. That is the email a D2 or NAIA assistant reads at 10 p.m. on a Tuesday and forwards to his head coach. Section 8 shows you the version for October of sophomore year, the one for June 1, the one for the week the season ends, and the one for the senior who is still unsigned in March.

The reel structure and the four emails in the Playbook are the same ones I give families for free when they ask me in a gym. The difference is you get them written down, in order, with the dates they go out.

Before you buy, the questions I get asked

Parents ask me the same questions over and over. Here are six of them with the honest answers, so you know what you are paying for before you pay for it.

"My son is a D1 player. Is this book for us?"

I hear this one the most. Here is what I say to that dad, every time.

I never tell anybody that they're not a D1 player, because everybody has potential. It's just a matter of how bad they want it. Genetics is a huge factor, obviously. But don't crush anybody's dreams. But the biggest thing is to be realistic and look at this as a 3 to 5 year plan, not a, I'm coming out of college now and I want to do a press conference and put my hat on. Because again, colleges are spending their time recruiting the other team's best player more than the high school student athlete that they know is going to take 2 to 3 years to develop and still could become a great player. But it's all about the win now.

If your son is a 4 or 5 star recruit, he does not need this book. His phone is already ringing. If he is not, and he still wants to play college ball, this book is the 3 to 5 year plan.
