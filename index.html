The Recruiting Playbook. Boys Basketball Edition.

Your son can play college basketball. Probably not where you think.

A family guide to college basketball recruiting, from a man who has watched it work and watched it fail for 30 years. $79.

A dad stopped me in a gym and told me his son wants to play in college, and he asked me what they should do. I get that question a lot, and I always answer it with questions of my own.

How are his grades?

What level is the goal, and why that level?

Has he researched any schools that would be a fit for the type of student athlete he is, and has he actually talked to any of them?

How many days a week is he working out on his own?

Is he a team player, or is he a scorer?

Most parents cannot answer half of those, and that is not a knock on them, because nobody ever told them those were the questions. That is why I wrote this book.

I have produced high school basketball events in Portland for 30 years, including the Nike Hoop Summit and the Les Schwab Invitational, and in that time I have helped a lot of families get their kids to every level of college basketball, D1 included, without ever charging them a dime. Some of those players are in the NBA, the WNBA and the NFL today, and I do not name them. I wrote this for the families whose son is not going to get a call from Duke, because most of the kids playing college basketball tonight never got that call either, and they are playing anyway.

You have to be honest and real about where your son is right now, and then he has to outwork everybody else. This book is the plan for doing both.

Here are the numbers, and then we can talk

Last year 539,883 boys played high school basketball. About 3.6 percent of them will play NCAA basketball at any level, and 1.1 percent will play Division I. Those are the NCAA's numbers, not mine.

It has also gotten harder in the last three years. This past spring, 2,605 Division I players went into the transfer portal, which is more than half of all the D1 players in the country. A college coach with one open spot can take a 21-year-old who has already produced in college games or a 17-year-old who produced in high school games, and he takes the 21-year-old every time. Then the roster is full, and your son is still waiting on a phone call that was never coming.

So when the travel ball coach says "D1" in front of you in April, be ready to ask him which D1 and what he is basing that on. If he cannot tell you, stay polite, say "OK, I hope that truly works out," and then go find out for yourself.

The other side of this is real too, and it is the reason I keep doing it. I have watched a kid land on a college roster that actually wanted him, play as a freshman, get film in his first year, and have a path up through the portal if he earned it and a degree either way, with his family not broke at the end of it. That is what this book is for.

What the chase costs

Nobody at the tournament is going to tell you this part, so I will.

In 2024 the average family spent $1,016 on their kid's main sport, according to the Aspen Institute's survey of 1,848 youth sports parents, and that number is up 46 percent in five years. Basketball parents were worse than average: they spent 105 percent more in 2024 than they did in 2019. The price of the chase doubled, and the number of D1 roster spots did not.

And that $1,016 is every kid in every sport, most of them nine years old, so a family chasing a scholarship is spending a lot more than that. A reporter tracked travel ball expenses this summer and came up with program fees of $800 to more than $1,000, one three-day showcase weekend at about $1,000 for one parent and one player, and a July of out-of-state live period events at roughly $6,000 for two people. Then you add the trainer and the streaming subscription at $25 a day so you can watch the games you could not drive to. He put a full season at about $10,000, and families told him they spend more than that.

Now add the back end. Say your son takes a preferred walk-on spot at an out-of-state D1 because the travel ball coach said he could. The College Board puts that at $31,880 a year in tuition and fees before he eats anything, and that buys a seat on the bench behind a 22-year-old transfer who already produced somewhere else.

Do the math on your own receipts tonight: four years of travel ball, a prep year if somebody talks you into one, and the walk-on tuition. Write that number down. Then ask yourself whether he could have been playing college basketball on scholarship money, at a school that called him in October, for a fraction of it.

That happens every single year. In 2024-25, 19,617 young men played NCAA basketball, and about 6,000 of them played D1. The rest played D2 and D3, and that does not even count NAIA and junior college. Most of the kids playing college basketball tonight are not D1, and most of them were not chasing it either. Their families found the door that was open.

What it costs your son

The money is the part you can count. The other part is harder.

It is the kid who was the best player on every team he ever played on, sitting in a gym in July and watching a coach get up and leave at halftime. It is a senior year spent waiting on a phone that does not ring, while the kid down the road, the one your son outplayed in February, signs with a D2 in November because his family said yes. It is the walk-on year you paid $31,880 for, where he practices every day, plays nine minutes all season, and comes home in May asking whether he was ever any good.

He was. The plan was wrong, not the kid.

Here is the other version of that story. The sophomore who starts at an NAIA school 90 minutes from home, plays 28 minutes a night against grown men, and has a stat line a D1 assistant can pull up in March. The D2 freshman on a 60 percent scholarship with academic money stacked on top, who gets real minutes and has a path up if he earns it and a degree either way. That is your son somewhere that wanted him, on a plan, with your family not broke.

I get asked about the preferred walk-on route every spring, and here is what I tell people. Choosing between a smaller school where your education is paid for and a preferred walk-on spot is a real issue, because the expectation is always to play right away and everybody believes in himself, but in reality it does not happen very often. You have to be willing to put in multiple years of work, or you end up trying to build something you can use to transfer somewhere else, because in my experience that is exactly what happens to a large percentage of preferred walk-ons.

It is April of senior year, and the phone has not rung

Here is the spring nobody plans for. Your son is a senior, he had a good year, and the coach at the school he wanted said nice things back in the fall. Then the season ended, the texts slowed down, and now it is April with no offer on the table. Meanwhile, a kid two towns over signed in November. Your son played against him twice, and you know how those games went.

I have watched that spring happen to a lot of families. They become desperate, because it affects the student athlete mentally, emotionally and physically, and most of the time it is because they were not realistic about where he could play and where he wanted to play. If they had started a lot earlier, they would have learned through the process that colleges at any level are pretty consistent in how they evaluate talent, and they should have gone and understood that. So some of those kids play and some do not, and a lot of them have to find some place they can fit if it is still their dream. A lot of them start to lose hope.

Read that last line again, because that is the cost. Not the money. The hope.

Here is why April is so quiet now. The D1 transfer portal opened April 7 this year and closed April 21, and for those 15 days every coach in the country was shopping for 21-year-olds with college film. Your son's film is against 17-year-olds. He was not losing to the kid two towns over. He was losing to a junior at another college who wanted a new school. That is not a reason to quit. It is a reason to know the calendar before your son is standing in it.

Some of you are reading this with a verbal commitment in hand and thinking it does not apply to you. There are too many variables when all you have is a verbal. Coaches change, an assistant leaves, the staff turns over, or another player your son is friends with decides to go somewhere else. Usually things change mightily, and other schools come into play. A verbal is a conversation. The early signing period this year ran November 11 through 18, and the regular period opens April 14. Until something is signed and the aid agreement is in writing, your son is unsigned, and unsigned is a status, not a plan.

Now the other side of it, because there is one. The spring is late, but it is not over. I have seen kids get offered right up until enrollment, because injuries happen, dropouts happen and transfers happen, and when a spot opens up the coach calls the student athlete he already knows is a good person who communicates well and works hard. You never really know when your opportunity is going to present itself, so be ready, and understand that what you do right now affects your future.

Being ready means something specific. It means a two-minute reel a coach will actually open, with full game film behind it. It means a list of 20 schools at four levels, D2, D3, NAIA and junior college included, with a name and an email for each one. It means a first email your son wrote himself, and a parent who understands that a D2 coach with a roster hole in May is a real door and not a consolation prize.

The Recruiting Playbook is that list, that reel, that email and that calendar, freshman year through signing day. If your son is already a senior, read Section 13 tonight. If he is a freshman, you have four years to make sure you never need Section 13 at all.

The film and the email

Two things get a kid recruited below the top 50 programs: film a coach will open and an email the coach answers. Two full sections cover them. Here is the short version.

Do not pay for a highlight reel

It is not needed. When a coach is interested, he is going to want full game film, and that speaks for itself. You are going to have bad plays too, and coaches want to see those bad plays and how you respond to them. A two-minute reel gets the door open, and the full game is what he actually watches. Section 6 gives you the two-minute structure and shows you how to send the full game link so the coach can find the bad plays himself.

What the coach is looking at

I have stood next to college coaches at events for 30 years, and what they look at is how a player responds to his teammates and to the situation the game is in. They are watching his composure, his enthusiasm, his hustle, his demeanor. Not the dunk and not the step-back three, but how he acts when the play goes wrong. A coach who sees your son sprint back after a turnover has learned more than any highlight could ever tell him.

Who writes the email

A parent tells me the kid will not write the email himself, and I tell them that he is probably not very motivated, then, because if he cannot write an email about something he is supposedly serious about, he is not getting up at 5 a.m. for the workout either. The email comes from your son, not from you and not from a service. Section 8 has the template, and your job is to proofread it and nothing else.

What goes in it

It is short. I am very interested in your program, and I think I could be a great fit. One thing about his best attribute as a teammate, a community member or a school member, something that shows his personality. Then he lets the film speak for itself. Four sentences, a link and his coach's phone number. That is the email a D2 or NAIA assistant reads at 10 at night and forwards to his head coach. Section 8 has the version for October of sophomore year, the one for June 1, the one for the week the season ends, and the one for the senior who is still unsigned in March.

These are the same things I tell families for free when they stop me in a gym. The difference is that you get them written down, in order, with the dates they go out.

The questions I get asked before anyone buys

"My son is a D1 player. Is this book for us?"

I hear this one the most. I never tell anybody he is not a D1 player, because everybody has potential, and it is really a matter of how badly he wants it. Genetics is a huge factor, obviously, but I do not crush anybody's dreams. The biggest thing is to be realistic and look at this as a 3 to 5 year plan, not as a press conference where he puts on a hat the day he leaves high school. Colleges are spending their time recruiting the other team's best player, not the high school student athlete they know is going to take two or three years to develop, because it is all about winning now.

If your son is a 4 or 5 star recruit, he does not need this book, because his phone is already ringing. If he is not, and he still wants to play college ball, this is the 3 to 5 year plan.

"Is this a recruiting service? Will you contact coaches for us?"

No. It is a $79 PDF. Nobody emails a coach for you, and nobody puts your son on a list. I wrote it because I watched what the paid services did to a family I knew. They spent the money on a recruiting company that basically told them to keep doing what they were already doing, and they never got the offer they were promised. The kid ended up at a smaller school and dropped out right away because it was not big enough for his dream, when he could have had a great experience if he had stuck with it. The expectations that recruiting company set, and the lies they told, made him lose confidence in his own ability.

This book teaches your son to send the email himself, because a coach can tell in one paragraph whether the kid wrote it, and he answers the kid.

"We already paid for a showcase and a service. Is it too late?"

It is not too late to stop paying, and it is almost never too late for a spot. Section 7 is the calendar from freshman year to signing day, and it tells you what "be ready" means in the month you are in right now.

"Does it cover NIL and the transfer portal?"

Two sections do. Here is the short version. It is all about getting film now, because with the portal being what it is, college players are sticking around. There is a need at every level, but your son needs to play, and if he does not play, he does not have film. That is the end of the line, especially if he is not a 4 or 5 star.

"We are not in Oregon. Does it still apply?"

Yes. The recruiting calendar, the contact rules, the portal windows and the scholarship math are NCAA, NAIA and junior college rules, and they are the same in Texas as they are in Portland. The one place I use an Oregon example is the high school NIL rule, and I tell you where to find your own state's version.

"What should we do the night we buy it?"

Read sections 1 through 5 before you spend another dollar. Then do your research on which school is a great fit academically, socially and economically. Even if he has to pay to play somewhere or take out loans, understand that the time he puts in is going to be the same at any level: the workouts, the study tables, the practices, the off-season requirements. Make sure this is truly what he wants to do.
